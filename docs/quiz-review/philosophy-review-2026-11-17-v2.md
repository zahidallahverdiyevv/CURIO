Review every question independently. Flag factual errors, disputed or time-sensitive claims, multiple defensible answers, weak distractors, inaccurate explanations and difficulty mismatches. Pay particular attention to answer-length clues, grammatical clues, implausible distractors, absolute wording and answer-position patterns. Do not assume the marked answer is correct. ## Logic & Argument

Quiz ID: `logic-argument`

### 01 · Easy

What is a premise in an argument?

**A.** The claim being supported

**B.** The speaker's emotional reaction

**C.** The argument's publication date

**D.** A claim offered in support

**Marked answer: D. A claim offered in support**

**Explanation:** Premises are statements presented as reasons for accepting a conclusion.

---

### 02 · Easy

When is a deductive argument valid?

**A.** Its premises are accepted by most listeners

**B.** True premises cannot accompany a false conclusion

**C.** Its explanation contains several supporting examples

**D.** Its conclusion happens to be a true statement

**Marked answer: B. True premises cannot accompany a false conclusion**

**Explanation:** Validity concerns truth-preserving structure, not whether the actual premises are true.

---

### 03 · Easy

What makes a deductive argument sound?

**A.** It is valid and its premises are true

**B.** Its premises repeat the conclusion exactly

**C.** It is valid and its conclusion is unpopular

**D.** Its conclusion has several different interpretations

**Marked answer: A. It is valid and its premises are true**

**Explanation:** Soundness combines validity with the truth of the premises.

---

### 04 · Easy

Which fallacy attacks the person rather than the relevant argument?

**A.** Circular reasoning

**B.** Equivocation

**C.** False dilemma

**D.** Ad hominem

**Marked answer: D. Ad hominem**

**Explanation:** An ad hominem substitutes an irrelevant personal attack for evaluating the reasons offered.

---

### 05 · Easy

Which term describes assigning two meanings to the same crucial word?

**A.** Generalization

**B.** Equivocation

**C.** Conjunction

**D.** Deduction

**Marked answer: B. Equivocation**

**Explanation:** Equivocation can make an argument seem connected while its key term changes meaning.

---

### 06 · Medium

If every whale is a mammal and Luna is a whale, what follows?

**A.** Every mammal is a whale

**B.** Luna is not a mammal

**C.** Some whales are not mammals

**D.** Luna is a mammal

**Marked answer: D. Luna is a mammal**

**Explanation:** The premises license applying the general mammal classification to Luna.

---

### 07 · Medium

If P implies Q, and P is true, what inference yields Q?

**A.** Modus tollens

**B.** Affirming the consequent

**C.** Denying the antecedent

**D.** Modus ponens

**Marked answer: D. Modus ponens**

**Explanation:** Modus ponens moves from a conditional and its antecedent to its consequent.

---

### 08 · Medium

If P implies Q and Q is true, why does P not necessarily follow?

**A.** Q may hold for another reason

**B.** A true Q makes the conditional false

**C.** The implication must be reversible

**D.** Q proves that P is necessary

**Marked answer: A. Q may hold for another reason**

**Explanation:** Affirming the consequent mistakes a sufficient condition for a necessary one.

---

### 09 · Medium

What is the negation of 'All swans are white' in classical logic?

**A.** At least one swan is not white

**B.** Every swan is not white

**C.** At least one swan is white

**D.** No nonwhite animals are swans

**Marked answer: A. At least one swan is not white**

**Explanation:** A universal claim is false if there is a counterexample within its subject class.

---

### 10 · Medium

What does a false dilemma do?

**A.** It uses a term with two different meanings

**B.** It assumes a conclusion inside a premise

**C.** It presents too few options as exhaustive

**D.** It offers an example unrelated to the topic

**Marked answer: C. It presents too few options as exhaustive**

**Explanation:** A false dilemma overlooks relevant alternatives between or beyond the offered choices.

---

### 11 · Hard

If P implies Q and Q is false, what follows in classical logic?

**A.** P is true

**B.** The conditional P implies Q is false

**C.** P is false

**D.** Q implies P

**Marked answer: C. P is false**

**Explanation:** Modus tollens licenses denying the antecedent when the consequent of a true conditional is false.

---

### 12 · Hard

Which condition makes a material conditional P → Q false?

**A.** P is false and Q is true

**B.** P is true and Q is false

**C.** P is true and Q is true

**D.** P is false and Q is false

**Marked answer: B. P is true and Q is false**

**Explanation:** In classical propositional logic, this is the conditional's sole falsifying truth-value combination.

---

### 13 · Hard

Some artists are teachers. Every teacher is a reader. What follows?

**A.** Some readers are not teachers

**B.** Every artist is a reader

**C.** Some artists are readers

**D.** Every reader is an artist

**Marked answer: C. Some artists are readers**

**Explanation:** The artists who are teachers belong to the reader class; the premises do not classify every artist or reader.

---

### 14 · Hard

How does 'No A are B' relate to 'No B are A' in standard class logic?

**A.** They contradict each other when B exists

**B.** The first entails the second only if A exists

**C.** They are logically equivalent

**D.** The second implies that every A is a B

**Marked answer: C. They are logically equivalent**

**Explanation:** Both statements assert that the two classes have no members in common.

---

### 15 · Hard

Why can a logically valid argument still be unpersuasive as evidence?

**A.** Its conclusion does not repeat P

**B.** Its premises may lack support

**C.** Its validity needs an inductive proof

**D.** Its form depends on the speaker

**Marked answer: B. Its premises may lack support**

**Explanation:** Validity is conditional on the premises; evidence is still needed for accepting them.

## Knowledge & Belief

### 01 · Easy

Which branch of philosophy studies knowledge?

**A.** Ethics

**B.** Epistemology

**C.** Political philosophy

**D.** Aesthetics

**Marked answer: B. Epistemology**

**Explanation:** Epistemology studies knowledge, justification and related questions about belief.

---

### 02 · Easy

What distinguishes believing a claim from its being true?

**A.** Belief is an attitude; truth concerns the claim

**B.** Belief concerns the future; truth concerns the past

**C.** Belief concerns numbers; truth concerns images

**D.** Belief requires writing; truth requires speaking

**Marked answer: A. Belief is an attitude; truth concerns the claim**

**Explanation:** People can believe true or false propositions; conviction does not itself establish truth.

---

### 03 · Easy

Which source of knowledge depends on another person's report?

**A.** Introspection

**B.** Deduction

**C.** Testimony

**D.** Perception

**Marked answer: C. Testimony**

**Explanation:** Testimony includes learning through what others tell or communicate to us.

---

### 04 · Easy

What is skepticism in an epistemological context?

**A.** Accepting whatever most people believe

**B.** Treating every belief as a moral duty

**C.** Questioning whether knowledge claims are justified

**D.** Explaining how political institutions are elected

**Marked answer: C. Questioning whether knowledge claims are justified**

**Explanation:** Skepticism challenges knowledge claims or their justification in various domains and degrees.

---

### 05 · Easy

What is an a priori justification traditionally understood to be independent of?

**A.** The meanings of the concepts involved

**B.** Particular sensory experience

**C.** The use of a valid inference

**D.** The subject's ability to understand a claim

**Marked answer: B. Particular sensory experience**

**Explanation:** A priori justification is contrasted with justification dependent on experience, though its exact scope is debated.

---

### 06 · Medium

What do Gettier cases challenge?

**A.** Knowledge as incompatible with any sensory evidence

**B.** True belief as a necessary condition for knowledge

**C.** Justification as irrelevant to the concept of knowledge

**D.** Justified true belief as a sufficient account

**Marked answer: D. Justified true belief as a sufficient account**

**Explanation:** Gettier-style cases describe justified true beliefs whose truth seems too lucky to count as knowledge.

---

### 07 · Medium

Why is a lucky correct guess not normally treated as knowledge?

**A.** Its truth was established by too much supporting evidence

**B.** Its certainty exceeds the strength required for knowledge

**C.** Its truth lacks the required epistemic connection

**D.** Its content is justified merely by matching the answer

**Marked answer: C. Its truth lacks the required epistemic connection**

**Explanation:** Knowledge requires more than accidentally arriving at a true answer; theories differ on the additional conditions.

---

### 08 · Medium

Which case most clearly illustrates confirmation bias?

**A.** Suspending judgment while an independent source is consulted

**B.** Testing the favored explanation against a strong alternative

**C.** Giving contrary evidence more weight after checking it

**D.** Seeking supporting evidence while neglecting objections

**Marked answer: D. Seeking supporting evidence while neglecting objections**

**Explanation:** Confirmation bias favors information supporting an existing belief and discounts contrary information.

---

### 09 · Medium

What does fallibilism allow?

**A.** Knowledge without an infallible guarantee

**B.** Knowledge of a claim despite knowing it is false

**C.** Justification based on the strength of personal conviction

**D.** Truth determined by agreement within a social group

**Marked answer: A. Knowledge without an infallible guarantee**

**Explanation:** Fallibilists reject the demand that knowing requires eliminating every possibility of error.

---

### 10 · Medium

What is the problem of induction concerned with?

**A.** Explaining why contradictions cannot both hold

**B.** Checking whether an argument preserves truth

**C.** Deducing conclusions from the meanings of terms

**D.** Justifying inferences beyond observed cases

**Marked answer: D. Justifying inferences beyond observed cases**

**Explanation:** Past observations do not deductively guarantee unobserved or future cases; the challenge is to explain their support.

---

### 11 · Hard

Why is 'the thermometer is reliable because it agrees with itself' inadequate as a calibration test?

**A.** Repeated agreement does not establish accuracy

**B.** Instruments are accurate whenever they give stable readings

**C.** Agreement with a preferred prediction is the calibration standard

**D.** Repeated agreement establishes accuracy without comparison

**Marked answer: A. Repeated agreement does not establish accuracy**

**Explanation:** A consistently biased instrument can repeat the same incorrect result; independent standards are needed.

---

### 12 · Hard

A witness is honest but has poor visibility. Which distinction matters?

**A.** Sincerity and perceptual reliability

**B.** Knowledge and acquaintance

**C.** Truth and logical necessity

**D.** Validity and soundness

**Marked answer: A. Sincerity and perceptual reliability**

**Explanation:** Honest reporting does not guarantee accurate perception under difficult conditions.

---

### 13 · Hard

What distinguishes reliabilism from requiring accessible reasons for every belief?

**A.** A process is reliable when the believer feels confident in it

**B.** Reliable belief-forming processes can justify beliefs

**C.** A person's conscious access to reasons is sufficient for reliability

**D.** A reliable belief must result from a consciously checked argument

**Marked answer: B. Reliable belief-forming processes can justify beliefs**

**Explanation:** Reliabilist accounts emphasize processes that tend to produce true beliefs, rather than requiring reasons fully available to reflection.

---

### 14 · Hard

A stopped clock shows the correct time when someone checks it. Which epistemic concern does this illustrate?

**A.** Justification is incompatible with beliefs about time

**B.** A correct conclusion establishes a reliable method

**C.** An unreliable source makes each statement false

**D.** A true belief can arise through luck

**Marked answer: D. A true belief can arise through luck**

**Explanation:** The match is accidental relative to the faulty information source, resembling concerns central to Gettier-style examples.

---

### 15 · Hard

What is the principal challenge of an infinite regress of justification?

**A.** Support is established by repeating the initial belief

**B.** Each supporting reason seems to need further support

**C.** An argument's conclusion is true whenever its premises are sincere

**Explanation:** Regress arguments motivate competing foundationalist, coherentist and other accounts of justification.
